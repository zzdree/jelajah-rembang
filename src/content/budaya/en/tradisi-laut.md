For the coastal people of Rembang, the sea is more than a place to earn a living. It is a neighbour owed respect, and that respect is expressed in ceremonies handed down through generations. The largest is **Sedekah Laut**, known in some places as **Lomban**. Its essence is simple: thanks for the sea's yield, and a plea to be kept from harm.

## The sequence of a sea offering

Sedekah Laut is held every year in the month of Sura, in stages. It opens with **Nyekar**, a visit to ancestral graves. Then comes **Tirakatan**, a night of shared prayer. The peak is **Larung**, when offerings are set adrift on the sea. After that comes **Ruwatan**, a cleansing from what is thought to bring misfortune. The prayers are led by a *modin*, who recites the **Rajah Kala Cakra** mantra.

At **Laut Bonang, in Lasem district**, the rite is a crowded annual fixture. In **Sarang district** the celebration runs up to four days and is said to cost around Rp 50 million, since it involves many boats, offerings, and performances.

## Earth offerings and village cleansing

In farming villages, gratitude takes the form of **Sedekah Bumi** or **Bersih Desa**. Residents bring in the harvest, hold a shared prayer, and clean the village. The shape differs from the sea tradition, but the logic is the same: an admission that fortune comes from beyond human effort alone.

> The sea and the fields teach the same lesson. Fortune is not entirely ours, and so it deserves to be shared in thanks.

## Washing the Bende Becak

One of the most distinctive traditions is **Penjamasan Bende Becak**, the ritual washing of Sunan Bonang's sacred gong. It is held in the month of Selo in Desa Bonang. The gong is believed to be a relic of Sunan Bonang, and its washing is a moment the whole village awaits.

## Hauls, processions, and festivals

Coastal tradition also lives on through the **Haul Mbah Sambu**, an annual commemoration that draws many pilgrims. There is also the **Kirab Budaya Mak Co Tian Siang Sheng Bo**, a cultural procession honouring a Chinese figure. In newer form come the **Lasem Batik Carnival**, the **Festival Lasem**, and the **Festival Gang Boen Toe**, which repackage tradition as public celebration.

What is striking is how these rites keep being reread. Some residents understand them as worship, some as cultural preservation, and some as a mark of village identity. All three readings coexist, none cancelling the others.
