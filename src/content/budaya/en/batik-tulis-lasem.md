Lasem batik is coastal batik, *batik pesisir*. It grew in a port town, far from the palace walls, and so it never obeyed the strict rules that govern the batik of Solo and Yogyakarta. Court batik demands fixed colours and fixed motifs; Lasem batik stays open. Anyone may wear it, from noble families to traders and labourers, from Javanese residents to Chinese ones. That openness is what let Lasem develop a visual language found nowhere else.

## The red of getih pitik

Lasem batik is best known for its colour: bold, dense, and confident. Its red has its own name, *getih pitik*, meaning chicken's blood. The name describes the deep red tone produced from dyes mixed to a particular measure. Other warm shades appear too, among them *bang bangan getih pitik*, one of the strongest markers of the style.

## Three lands in a single cloth

The name Tiga Negeri, Three Lands, refers to how one cloth was dyed in three separate places. The red was worked in Lasem, the blue in Pekalongan, and the brown *soga* in Solo. A variant, Empat Negeri or Four Lands, adds a fourth town. This cross-town arrangement shows that batik has long been a trading network, not merely a household craft.

## Motifs from two worlds

The motifs bring two traditions together. From the Chinese world come the *naga* (dragon), the *burung hong* (phoenix), and Chinese characters. From the Javanese world come *Latohan*, *Watu Pecah*, and *Lokchan*, often compared with Java's *Sekar Jagad*. Neither closes off the other; both blend on the same cloth.

> Lasem's red is not simply a colour. It is a sign that this port has always welcomed whoever arrives carrying a new shade.

## From Champa to today

Legend ties the arrival of batik to a Champa princess named Na Li Ni, in the era of Zheng He's voyages around 1405 to 1433. This story survives as folklore, and not every historian accepts it as a settled record. What is better documented is that Chinese-owned batik workshops once flourished, with a golden period in the 1860s when batik was the second trade after opium. Lasem cloth was exported to Singapore and Sri Lanka until the 1970s.

Indonesian batik was inscribed by UNESCO in 2009. In 2024 the Lasem batik trade archives were named by ANRI as Memory of the Collective Nation and nominated for UNESCO's MOWCAP register. Around 80 artisans still work across the regency today, keeping a colour that has never left its port town.
